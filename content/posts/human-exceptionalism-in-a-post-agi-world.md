## The Future of AI and Mathematics

I started my degree in mathematics this year, mostly for the fun of the subject and without much idea of what I really wanted to do after university. On the second day of university, OpenAI released [722 papers](https://openai.com/index/sharing-ai-progress-in-mathematics/) on many important open problems in Mathematics, including the Quasi-Riemann Hypothesis, a sub-problem of the larger Riemann Hypothesis that got me interested in pure mathematics in the first place. 

I think I was able to internalise this pretty quickly, and because I didn't have a huge amount of interest in going into academia, I was fairly quickly able to accept that I will never be better than AI at maths, which still, was fairly soul crushing (since it is a skill I had prided myself on for most my life).  I don't believe many of my friends outside of maths have undergone this realisation, and it's entirely plausible that they won't given significant government regulation in the near-future (which seems somewhat plausible at this point). 
[^1]

It wasn't the first time I had "realised" this, after all, lab leaders and AI researchers can't keep quiet about how AGI will be the last human invention, which also made sense to me logically. Still, I think seeing it actually happen in mathematics and to my professors made it seem real. In a sense, it was the first time I had truly felt the AGI, even if I could cognitively understand the implications of this technology.

### Handoverism and Crisis of Meaning

I believe several of these moments will come soon for other fields without government intervention or voluntary action from the labs. The most obvious are in theoretical physics, engineering, law, quantitative finance and machine learning, which all include verifiable tasks that can be easily trained on through RL. I am also quite worried about a possible intelligence explosion in 2027, which may result in these moments for essentially every (economically viable) cognitive task humans participate in.

![AI progress on mathematics benchmarks as measured by Epoch AI](/images/posts/human-exceptionalism-in-a-post-agi-world/g1b2muwawaac372-d2ce7a.jpg)

There is no doubt in my mind that this would trigger a large-scale crisis of meaning among knowledge workers and academics, which make up most of western civilisation. Unlike many blue-collar workers, knowledge workers often tie their work to their identity, especially those in humanities, which may very well lead to large civil unrest. I also think this would be the appropriate reaction to such a change, as it would mean that skills people had worked their lives to develop would no longer be of use to society.

This pursuit of superintelligence is extremely dangerous, but even if it goes well, many people question the future "utopia" that many of the AI CEOs envision. In particular, *Dario Amodei* of Anthropic published an essay titled [Machines of Loving Grace](https://https://darioamodei.com/essay/machines-of-loving-grace) highlights a world where the economy and all meaningful infrastructure is run by AI, and he makes reference to the Richard Brautigan poem "All Watched Over By Machines Of Loving Grace", which echoes this worldview.

I was particularly stricken by Jon Stewart's repudiation of this Utopian vision on [The Daily Show](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lnLDjXy2kRA) and I find that many prominent voices in the media and policy (Ezra Klein, Jon Ossoff, Ruben Gallego, David Robinson) also aren't sympathetic (for good reason) towards the handoverist ideology of Silicon Valley, which seems to be especially prominent at Anthropic.

<details>
<summary>Handoverism</summary>

Handoverism is the belief that humanity should willingly transfer governance and decision-making authorities to superintelligent AI systems once they are shown to be aligned with human values. It believes that having society controlled by a benevolent AI would increase the quality of life for all humans on Earth, allowing us to enjoy the benefits of such systems without the worry of monopolisation of this technology.

</details>

### Human Selfishness as a Bottleneck for Progress

 In this post, I want to examine more nuanced approaches to handoverism, which requires assuming that there are superintelligent systems that are under our control (at least in where they are deployed). Therefore, from now on, I will assume the case where alignment is solved, which may very well be impossible, in which case the pursuit of any superintelligent systems should be completely banned everywhere.

It is completely obvious to want to stay in control of the planet and preserve your self-worth, and therefore when tech CEOs seem to be unilaterally deciding that we are going to head straight towards total human disempowerment, people will start to take very extreme issues on the topic.  I also believe that whatever the outcome, it needs to be a democratic decision, and certainly not a decision made by 2-3 companies in San Francisco.

However, the more I think to myself about the promise of such a technology in science, it becomes harder to argue that handover in certain sectors wouldn't benefit more people than it would harm. I think of possible advances in medicine, engineering, science and an economy managed better by superintelligent systems. This applies to a lot of knowledge work too, and I am not super convinced that people draw meaning from working at a bank or venture capital firm. 

The full automation of technological progress could have immense benefits in fighting against climate change, helping people out of poverty, ending world hunger and solving many other difficult issues that have plagued humanity. I believe that while sad, the benefits here are simply too great to keep people as the sole contributors in this sector. As a result, I expect even in the case that the technology is given the regulation it desperately needs, most STEM fields will effectively go away.

![](/images/posts/human-exceptionalism-in-a-post-agi-world/pasted-f0dcd6.png)

I know many people who imbue their work in scientific fields with a lot of meaning. Those who want to discover, create great new technologies and products that can truly benefit humanity, and they have worked their lives towards these goals. Still, when I think of starving and sick children and those who can't get access to energy or water, I believe that simply pushing this to the side for the sake of meaning of a select group of people (who are typically quite well-off already) is quite selfish.

Furthermore, I think advancements in robotics could lead to a more humane outcome in blue-collar and manufacturing work, and I think the people in those lines of work would benefit from increased automation. This, combined with advances in science could plausibly lead to a larger abundance of goods and services to everyone, which could then be broadly distributed to increase the quality of life in this future society.

Still, handing off more and more control to these systems would result in the similar disempowerment of humanity. I believe it is important to keep the world human, and for AI to focus on delivering larger amounts of material goods to make the human experience better by greatly reducing poverty and sickness, among other things. However, I do not think it would be particularly wise to deploy agents into the financial system, arts, legal and political systems, which do not return tangible results to people, and are very much inventions rather than discoveries.

## Envisioning a Human Future

The thought that people want their kids to inherit a better, more caring world is not a radical one, yet, it seems like what the AI industry is doing is antithetical to that. It is therefore important to define what we want to get out of this transformative technology and try to draw red lines what we should allow autonomous systems to do. 

The majority of what people experience in the world is not natural, you read this on a screen designed by humans, probably in a room made by humans. In some cases, this has a very material benefit, like technology and medicine, which seek to remedy concrete facts about the world through a tangible means. However, the vast majority of work does not share this characteristic, the financial system or legal system do not create tangible measures of progress and as a result it is not clear to me that the deployment of superintelligent systems to automate this work would do more good than harm.

A much clearer cut example is in the arts, while I personally do not feel threatened by AI music (yet), freelance designers and commercial musicians have already been largely disrupted by image and music generation models. While the entertainment industry is quite large, I don't think it is a pressing issue to "solve" music or art for economic means. In a sense, these things which up to now have been truly uniquely human should remain as such, as they are the driving forces of culture. 

The types of jobs that should be automated can be visualised as a relatively small Goldilocks zone of professions that are useful and physical, (i.e. not social jobs like teaching, law, etc). Such a chart can be seen below:

![chart of what I perceive to be useful/physical jobs](/images/posts/human-exceptionalism-in-a-post-agi-world/the-plot-ea5eb7.png "{w=75%}")

This plot shows what I think would ideally be the jobs that would be given up to AI (which is funny, because they are some of the harder and more sought after jobs in today's market). These are the jobs that would have a net positive from being automated, as the trade off of loss-of-meaning is far less consequential than the scientific discoveries and lives saved through automating dangerous jobs.

In the top right are jobs that could be useful to automate, but only have much of their value because of their societal implications, without doing much that is actually tangible to uplift people. While I am not against leveraging AI in legal or policy positions, I believe that these sectors should remain controlled by humans. These are also jobs that people often tie to their identity, which would mitigate the unemployment and societal degradation from too large a hand-off.

On the left are the professions that are only important because they are human or have no leverage in the economy, things like sports, philosophy and history are included here, and I think that again AI can be used as a tool in history to demystify old documents or gather sources, but the importance of the profession remains largely in a social desire to understand what and who came before us.

However, it is clear in such an economy, things would be radically different, and this calls for a general restructuring of the social contract and western economic systems, especially the current capitalistic system that we live under. ### The Future of Capitalism and Human Achievement

At the end of the day, I think those who have really thought about it have realised that this technology is incompatible with the current form of distributing resources. If managed poorly, it will certainly result in a permanent underclass and the greatest difference in wealth ever (even if the floor was much higher than it is today).  Letting the market decide what to do in this case would simply mean the displacement of all human labour.

This was put quite well in an interview with Anthropic researcher Tao Lin, who said "I think that it’s very likely that pretty much all humans on Earth will become permanently  unemployable". Obviously, this is an undesirable outcome, and the labs have also realised this, even if it hasn't shifted their goals[^2]. I think, still, that the promises of this technology mandate that we build it (if it can be kept safe), and shunning this promise is going to cause millions of people to die or remain impoverished.

If you look at the Anthropic IPO advert (which I thought was beautifully done), the pitches for AI are very much that they will greatly accelerate scientific discovery and help humanity solve its greatest challenges, empowering us to do more things and better things. I think this framing is exactly where this technology needs to go, and falls in the bottom right quadrant of my chart, coincidentally.  However, it is clear that this is not possible under a completely free-market system, as the competitive pressures of the market will lead to this technology being deployed in every sector to maximise productivity. 

This is the end of the long game of capitalism, and in its place, I hope a new system that aims to maximise human experience over shareholder value will emerge from the many failures that this system brought us. In any case, whatever emerges, it is clear that this current system is not fit for when a more intelligent being exists on the planet, and if we want a human future, we must actively think about what comes next.
