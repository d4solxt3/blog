The Riemann Hypothesis is the holy grail of Mathematics, it is a problem that inspires people (including myself to some degree) to pursue higher mathematics. It connects the irregular spacing of the prime numbers to the zeroes of a continuous, complex function. In the eyes of many, the most beautiful in all mathematics is the zeta function, due to its magnitude of importance in spite of its utter simplicity, a notion tied to the ethos of pure mathematics. 

Prime numbers are the indivisible building blocks of mathematics. People have wondered since the dawn of mathematics if there is any music that can be heard to their apparently noisy distribution. It is a fundamental question to the nature of numbers, and looking at them, it feels like being in the presence of one of the inexplicable secrets of creation. In the mid-19th century, German mathematician G.F.B Riemann observed that the frequency of the prime numbers was related to the behaviour of an elaborate function $\zeta (s) = \frac{1}{1^s} + \frac{1}{2^s} + \frac{1}{3^s} + ... + \frac{1}{n^s}$ which has since been named the *Riemann-Zeta function*. 

This function is connects directly to the distribution of the prime numbers through Euler's product formula, which rewrites all positive integers as an infinite product on all primes. The Riemann hypothesis asserts that all non-trivial solutions of the equation $\zeta (s) = 0$ lie on a vertical line $\text{Re(s)} = \frac{1}{2}$ on the complex plane, that the frequency of primes is governed by a simple, seemingly unremarkable function. A proof to Riemann's claim has stood as the most important, coveted problem in not just number theory, but all of mathematics, and as of writing this proof, it has been proven by Levent Alpöge and Claude.

As a child, I had always been interested in mathematics, I first entered the British Mathematics Olympiad (BMO) at 13 years old, and had become interested in further mathematics slightly later. I read and explored different worlds of maths, inexplicably connected. Half a decade ago, I first met Marcus Du Sautoy, an Oxford University number theorist who became popular through his book *The Music of the Primes*, which detailed the history of the Riemann Hypothesis. I was awe-struck by the problem, but was pessimistic that it would ever get solved in my lifetime. I thought that maybe I should become a mathematician, and try my hand at it, to try peer into the spiral of Riemann, Gauss and Euler, and look into the eye of the universe. 

Then, arguing with my parents, weighing my possible career prospects, and having a genuine interest in engineering and building things, I decided to pursue aerospace engineering, and forgot about mathematics for a while. I still found it fun (I obviously had to study maths for my aerospace course and previous research) but I became disillusioned with the prospect of pursuing research mathematics after [AlphaGeometry](https://deepmind.google/blog/alphageometry-an-olympiad-level-ai-system-for-geometry/) and realising the pace of AI progress in mathematics was going to far outpace my own. 

Still, I thought Riemann was far out of reach, partially due to believing that the solutions to Riemann would have required developing a new field of mathematics, similar to the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, which I, along with most mathematicians was wrong about. I had timelines to Riemann far after superhuman-AI, holding it as one of those fundamental problems of the universe that would certainly be out of reach for us. If you had told me I wouldn't see a solution to Riemann in my lifetime, it would have seemed completely reasonable to me. Who would have thought that the convex hull of known mathematics contains such riches? 

Uniquely, unlike Navier-Stokes or the OpenAI maths drop with Quasi-Riemann among other proofs, I felt a sense of awe when I first read that Riemann fell. Perhaps it is because this is really the holy-grail of mathematics, it could also be because I had become more adjusted to the capabilities of these models, but when I introspect, it does feel like the wonder of seeing a proof of the Riemann hypothesis simply trumps the rational fears I should have about the growing capabilities of these models and what this means to the future. I am thankful to Alpöge, Anthropic, and the rest of humanity who got us to this point where song of the primes plays for all to hear, and I hope we take this as an example of what the future could look like, an age of miracles.
